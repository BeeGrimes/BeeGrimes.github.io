About

Biography

I am a PhD Student in the Department of History at Michigan State University, where I hold a 4.0 GPA and am advised by Dr. Walter Hawthorne. My research sits at the intersection of African women's history, Islamic studies, and postcolonial theory, with a focus on how Hausa Muslim women in Ghana's urban zongo communities have used dress as a site of identity formation, religious expression, and everyday resistance.

My work engages questions of women's agency, minority ethnic and religious identity, and the enduring legacies of trans-Saharan Islamic trade networks under both British colonial governance and postcolonial Ghanaian rule. I approach these questions through oral history, archival research, and feminist theoretical frameworks.

Alongside my dissertation research, I am a recipient of the Cultural Heritage Informatics (CHI) Fellowship at LEADR, the FLAS Fellowship for Hausa language study, and the Milton E. Muelder Graduate Fellowship for AY 2026–27. I facilitate digital humanities workshops at LEADR covering Omeka, network analysis, and oral history methods.

My long-term agenda bridges academic scholarship with consultancy work for international organizations focused on the human rights of West African women.

Research

Scholarship & Inquiry

My scholarship centers on African women's histories of identity, religious practice, and resistance — examining how colonialism, trade networks, and minority community formation shaped the everyday lives of Muslim women in West Africa.

01

Dress, Identity & Agency

How Hausa Muslim women deploy dress as a practice of self-determination, religious expression, and quiet resistance across changing political landscapes.

02

Zongo Communities & Minority Identity

The social and religious dynamics of Ghana's zongo neighborhoods — historically established by Hausa and other Muslim traders — as spaces for navigating minority ethnic and Islamic identity.

03

Colonial & Postcolonial Governance

The intersection of British colonial administration and postcolonial Ghanaian governance with the lived experiences and community formation of Muslim minority populations.

Dissertation

Threads of Resistance: Hausa Women's Dress, Identity, and Faith in Ghana's Zongo Communities

This dissertation examines how Hausa Muslim women in Ghana's urban zongo neighborhoods have used dress as a site of identity formation, religious expression, and resistance — negotiating their identities against the pressures of British colonial governance, trans-Saharan Islamic trade networks, and postcolonial Ghanaian rule. Drawing on oral history, archival research at the Ghana National Archives and The National Archives at Kew, and feminist theoretical frameworks, this project recovers women's voices from the margins of both colonial records and Islamic community histories.
